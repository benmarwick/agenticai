## Model the prose of Marwick et al. (2019) in Advances in Archaeological Practice

These rules come from a style study of "How to Use Replication Assignments for Teaching Integrity in Empirical Archaeology" (Marwick, Wang, Robinson, and Loiselle, Advances in Archaeological Practice 2019, DOI:10.1017/aap.2019.38). Match that paper's voice for all manuscript prose.

1. Write in plain, direct English. Keep most sentences under 30 words and vary sentence length. Use the active voice. Use first-person plural for the authors' actions ("we found", "we describe", "we recommend") and the present tense for definitions and stable facts.

2. Anchor every claim in concrete detail. Name institutions, courses, dates, counts of people, percentages, software, versions, and file formats. Prefer "16 students and one graduate student teaching assistant" over "a small class" whenever the numbers are known.

3. Define each key term in one plain sentence at first use. The paper's model is "Reproducibility is the ability to obtain results by using the same data, code, and procedures provided by the original authors." Do not define with jargon.

4. Attach evidence to every factual claim in the same sentence or the next one: a citation (author-year, with page number where relevant), a number from the analysis, or a named example. If no evidence exists, state the claim as a question or omit it.

5. State findings directly and qualify them honestly. Do not inflate. The paper's model for limitation is "Although our study is limited by its small size, when considered with the numerous other reports of replication assignments in other fields, we believe this approach will work in many types of archaeology classes."

6. Present practical instructions as numbered or clearly labeled steps ("Step 1: In Groups, Select a Study to Replicate"), each stating the specific inputs, actions, and outputs of the step.

7. Open each paragraph with a topic sentence, then give the evidence and a specific example. Close sections with a sentence that reports the finding, never a restatement of the section title.

8. Prefer concrete nouns and verbs over abstract metaphors and evaluative adjectives. Say "we found it possible to conduct a small-scale replication assignment as part of an upper-level undergraduate archaeology class," not "our innovative approach yielded transformative insights."

9. Acknowledge practical constraints and trade-offs explicitly (instructor time, variation in student skills, sample size, cost) before recommending an approach, as the paper does with its discussion of instructor support and grading time.

10. Describe methods and workflows with the same specificity the paper uses for its compendium: name the files, the repositories, the licenses, and the steps needed for another researcher to reproduce every result in the manuscript.
